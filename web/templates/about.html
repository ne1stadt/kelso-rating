The Kelso system

Kelso places every WCA personal best on one scale — the odds, in Elo units, of beating the personal best of a randomly drawn member of the 3x3 field — and adds those odds up across events. This page summarises the construction. The full argument, with validation, uncertainty and sensitivity analyses, is in the paper.

What the rating means

Kelso is a composite indicator of career-best standing: a deterministic function of a competitor's personal bests, built to satisfy explicit criteria rather than to estimate a parameter of a model. It is not an estimate of latent ability.

A difference of 400 points in an event rating is a tenfold difference in the odds of beating a random member of the 3x3 field. The overall rating sums those odds across a competitor's events:
where is the share of the reference field that the (linked) result in event beats.

The rating was built to five criteria:

  1. Event scale. Within an event, a rating is a monotone function of the share of a reference field the result beats, with a stated unit.
  2. Common scale. Events are placed on one reference field by a documented rule, and the strength of that rule is reported for each event.
  3. Aggregation. A higher event rating never lowers the overall rating, entering an event never lowers it, and the overall rating does not depend on the arbitrary origin of the scale.
  4. Stability. A competitor's rating does not change while their own results do not, except at announced renewals of the reference field.
  5. Feasibility. Ratings update within minutes of new results and recompute in full within an hour on one server.

So the rating ranks demonstrated achievement across events, with breadth credited and no event penalised, and its differences read as differences in summed odds against the reference field. It is not an estimate of general cubing ability, a probability that one competitor would beat another in some event, or a forecast of results in events not yet entered.

Kinch and Sum of Ranks charge every missing event, so they place all-round competitors higher; a mean of event ratings penalises adding a weaker event; the best event ignores breadth. Over all competitors every metric agrees closely with Kelso (Spearman 0.95–0.98), because most competitors hold one or two events. Event ceilings

The leading rating of each event depends on the size of the reference field and on how far the event's leader is ahead of their common group — not on the difficulty of the achievement. How it is calculated

1 · Personal bests

For each competitor and event: the best average (best mean of three for 6x6, 7x7 and FMC), and the best single for 3BLD, 4BLD, 5BLD and MBLD. An MBLD result is scored as its points plus the unused share of the hour, , and enters as so that smaller is better.

3 · Linking events to the 3x3 field

Raw percentiles are not comparable across events because the fields differ: the median 5BLD solver is a far faster 3x3 solver than the median 3x3 competitor. Every event is placed on the 3x3 field by equipercentile linking through its common group — the competitors with personal bests in both and 3x3:

  1. : the position of the result in the common group;
  2. : the 3x3 result at the same position among the same group's 3x3 results;
  3. : the share of the full 3x3 field that beats.

Every distribution is continuized with a Gaussian kernel on log results (kernel equating, von Davier, Holland & Thayer 2004), with Silverman's bandwidth and variance-preserving scaling:

Linking assumes that competitors at the same position in the two distributions of a common group are equally strong. Only 2x2 reaches the conventional correlation for a concordance (; 2x2 0.89, 4x4 0.86, OH 0.85); the blindfolded events are far below it (0.19–0.36). For most events the linking is therefore a documented scale alignment, a convention, not a measurement.

4 · Aggregation

Requiring that a higher event rating never lowers the overall rating, that events contribute separately as a sum (so entering an event never lowers it), and that adding a constant to every event rating adds it to the overall (independence from the origin 1200) leaves exactly one family of rules — the log-sum-exp (Hardy, Littlewood & Pólya 1934; Aczél 1966):

The choice is normative: no contest compares one competitor's results across events, so the overall rating is an Elo-scaled composite rather than an Elo rating. Reference field and updates

Event ratings are shares of a field, so they would drift whenever anyone else competes — unchanged personal bests lost a median of 4–30 points per event over 22 months. Following equating practice, the distributions are estimated once on a base population and then frozen. New results are rated against the frozen distributions as new members of the field, and the base is renewed annually at an announced date, with the shift of each event published.

Measured against the exact formula, interpolation error stayed below 0.6 points (below 0.01 for 99% of results). Evidence

  • Finishing orders. In 2.09 million pairs from 2025–26 rounds, event-rating differences computed from results up to 2024 predict who finishes ahead with a pooled logistic scale of 374 points per tenfold odds (95% CI 371–378), close to the Elo 400. The scale differs by event, from 235–305 for sighted cube events to 737–844 for 5BLD, MBLD, 4BLD and FMC, so equal rating differences do not mean equal head-to-head odds across events.
  • New events. As a predictor of the rating in an event entered later, Kelso ranks behind the mean and the best event rating (Spearman 0.387 vs 0.469 and 0.406), ahead of Sum of Ranks and Kinch — by design, its breadth term carries no information about the new event.
  • Sensitivity. The top of the ranking is stable under the bandwidth, the blindfolded format and K between 300 and 500 (rank-biased overlap ≥ 0.90). It changes substantially only when a defining choice is replaced: typical instead of best results, separate level and breadth constants, head-to-head rescaling, or K = 1300.
  • Uncertainty. The median width of a 95% interval is 274 points in the top 20, 148 in the top 100 and 47 in the top 1000 (conversion bootstrap). Below the leader, neighbouring ranks are usually not distinguishable.

How the data is gathered

Official results come from the WCA results export, regenerated daily. Competitions that are running, or have ended but are not yet in the export, are read from their public WCIF — the format that carries live results for both WCA Live and the WCA website's own scoretaking.

  • When. A cron job asks the WCA export endpoint for the export date every hour — a 1 KB request. Only when a new export appears (once a day) does the pipeline run.
  • Only what is needed. The export is a single zip, but each member is compressed separately and the directory sits at the end. With HTTP range requests the pipeline reads the directory and then only persons, competitions, results, countries and continents — about a third of the archive. Scrambles and individual attempts are never downloaded.
  • Fast build. Results are reduced to each competitor's best single and average per competition (6.98 M rows → 4.73 M). Every personal best that ever stood is rated through the frozen tables; because the overall rating is of a sum of odds, each competitor's whole history is a cumulative sum of odds increments.   • Zero-downtime publish. Each build writes a new SQLite file; a symlink is swapped atomically and the web server opens it read-only with immutable=1, so readers never see a half-written database and need no locks.
  • Live competitions. Nothing is polled in the background. When someone opens a running competition, the server fetches its public WCIF at most once every two minutes (once an hour after it ended), with a single request in flight per competition, and rates new personal bests against the frozen base. These are labelled estimates until the official export includes the competition.

Limitations

  • Linking conditions. Only 2x2 meets the threshold for a concordance, and conversions vary with cohort and region by 7–74 points. The relative placement of events is a documented convention.
  • Event scales. Rating differences translate into different head-to-head odds in different events.
  • Correlated events. Events that share skills receive independent breadth credit; the 17 events carry about 2.1 effective dimensions.
  • Personal bests and exposure. A personal best is the best of a varying number of attempts; frequent competitors have more chances. The rating credits the best result obtained, as the WCA rankings do.
  • Field dependence. Ratings depend on who else competes; under the frozen base this shows up once a year, at renewal.
  • Live estimates use unofficial results and treat each pending competition separately from other pending ones.
